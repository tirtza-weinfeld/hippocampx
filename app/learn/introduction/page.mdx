## Deterministic ODE Pipeline

### 1. We start with a vector field

$$
u : \mathbb{R}^d \times [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}^d
$$

This means: for every position $x$ and parameter $t$, it gives a vector $u_t(x)$.

So before any ODE, we just have a rule

$$
(x,t) \mapsto \text{velocity vector}.
$$

---

### 2. Introduce an unknown trajectory

We now look for a function

$$
X : [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}^d,
\quad t \mapsto X_t.
$$

This is a curve in space.

At this point, $X$ is completely unknown — it could be any path.

---

### 3. Impose the rule (ODE)

We require that at every $t$,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}X_t = u_t(X_t).
$$

Meaning:

* The trajectory is at position $X_t$.
* The field assigns velocity $u_t(X_t)$ there.
* The derivative of the trajectory equals that velocity.

This equation is the ODE.  
Vector field + "match velocity to field" = ODE.

---

### 4. Add an initial condition

The equation alone allows many trajectories.

So we choose a starting point:

$$
X_0 = x_0.
$$

Now the full problem is

$$
\frac{d}{dt}X_t = u_t(X_t),
\quad X_0 = x_0.
$$

This is an **initial value problem**.  
Under standard regularity conditions on $u$, there is a unique solution.

---

### 5. Define the flow (global solution map)

Instead of fixing one $x_0$, let it vary.

Define

$$
\psi : \mathbb{R}^d \times [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}^d,
\quad (x_0,t) \mapsto \psi_t(x_0)
$$

such that

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\psi_t(x_0) = u_t(\psi_t(x_0)),
\quad \psi_0(x_0) = x_0.
$$

Meaning:

* For each fixed $x_0$, the curve $t \mapsto \psi_t(x_0)$ solves the IVP.
* The flow collects all trajectories into one map.

For a chosen $x_0$:

$$
X_t = \psi_t(x_0).
$$

---

### 6. Often no closed-form expression

Assume $u : \mathbb{R}^d \times [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}^d$
is sufficiently regular (e.g. locally Lipschitz in $x$, continuous in $t$).

Then the initial value problem

$$
\frac{d}{dt}X_t = u_t(X_t),
\quad X_0 = x_0
$$

admits a unique solution

$$
X : [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}^d.
$$

That is:

There exists a function $X(t)$ satisfying the differential equation and the initial condition.

However, in general, this solution cannot be written in closed form.

Therefore, instead of computing the exact function $t \mapsto X_t$,
we compute approximations of its values on a discrete grid.

---

### 7. Discretization of the interval

Fix an integer $n \ge 1$.

Define the step size

$$
h := \frac{1}{n}.
$$

This partitions the interval $[0,1]$ into $n$ equal subintervals.

Define the grid

$$
t_k := k h,
\quad k = 0,1,\dots,n.
$$

Thus

$$
t_0 = 0,
\quad
t_n = 1.
$$

---

### 8. Discrete approximations

Introduce a sequence

$$
X_0, X_1, \dots, X_n \in \mathbb{R}^d,
$$

intended to approximate the exact solution values

$$
X(t_0), X(t_1), \dots, X(t_n).
$$

We impose

$$
X_0 := x_0.
$$

Here:

- $X(t_k)$ denotes the true solution.
- $X_k$ denotes its numerical approximation.
- The index $k$ is discrete.

---

### 9. Derivation of the explicit Euler scheme

Start from the definition of the derivative:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}X_t
=
\lim_{h \to 0}
\frac{X_{t+h} - X_t}{h}.
$$

Since

$$
\frac{d}{dt}X_t = u_t(X_t),
$$

replace the limit by a small finite step size $h$:

$$
\frac{X_{t+h} - X_t}{h}
\approx
u_t(X_t).
$$

Rearranging gives

$$
X_{t+h}
=
X_t
+
h\,u_t(X_t).
$$

Evaluating at $t = t_k$ yields the recursive scheme

$$
X_{k+1}
=
X_k
+
h\,u_{t_k}(X_k).
$$

This defines the explicit Euler method.

---

### 10. Euler algorithm (recursive form)

Given:

- vector field $u(x,t)$,
- initial state $x_0$,
- number of steps $n$,

define

$$
h = \frac{1}{n}.
$$

Set

$$
X_0 = x_0.
$$

For $k = 0,1,\dots,n-1$, compute

$$
t_k = k h,
$$

$$
X_{k+1}
=
X_k
+
h\,u_{t_k}(X_k).
$$

The sequence

$$
X_0, X_1, \dots, X_n
$$

approximates

$$
X(t_0), X(t_1), \dots, X(t_n).
$$

---

### 11. What this produces

This generates discrete values

$$
X_0, X_1, \dots, X_n,
$$

which approximate the continuous trajectory $X_t$.

Since

$$
X_t = \psi_t(x_0),
$$

these discrete points approximate the flow $\psi_t(x_0)$.

---

### 12. Convergence statement

Under standard smoothness assumptions on $u$,

$$
\max_{0 \le k \le n}
\| X_k - X(t_k) \|
\to 0
\quad \text{as } h \to 0.
$$

Thus the discrete trajectory converges to the true solution
as the mesh size decreases.

---

## Stochastic SDE / Diffusion Pipeline

### 13. Make the trajectory stochastic

Now $X_t$ is no longer deterministic.

For each fixed $t$, $X_t$ is a random variable.

Simulating twice can produce different paths.

---

### 14. Introduce Brownian motion

We add a stochastic process $W_t$ such that

$$
W_{t+h} - W_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, h I_d).
$$

Each small increment is Gaussian with variance proportional to $h$.

This provides the randomness.

---

### 15. Extend the infinitesimal update

Recall the deterministic infinitesimal form:

$$
X_{t+h} = X_t + h\,u_t(X_t).
$$

Add a stochastic term:

$$
X_{t+h}
=
X_t
+
h\,u_t(X_t)
+
\sigma_t (W_{t+h} - W_t).
$$

This defines a **stochastic differential equation**.

---

### 16. Symbolic SDE notation

It is written compactly as

$$
dX_t = u_t(X_t)\,dt + \sigma_t\,dW_t,
\quad X_0 = x_0.
$$

Important:

This $dX_t$ notation is symbolic shorthand for the infinitesimal update above.

---

### 17. No deterministic flow

Unlike ODEs, there is no deterministic flow $\psi_t$.

Even with fixed $X_0$, randomness in $W_t$ makes $X_t$ random.

An ODE is the special case $\sigma_t = 0$.

---

### 18. Euler–Maruyama simulation

Choose number of steps $n$ and set

$$
h = \frac{1}{n}.
$$

Initialize:

$$
X_0 = x_0.
$$

For

$$
t = 0, h, 2h, \dots, 1-h,
$$

update using

$$
X_{t+h}
=
X_t
+
h\,u_t(X_t)
+
\sqrt{h}\,\sigma_t\,\varepsilon_t,
\quad
\varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d).
$$

---

### 19. What this produces

This generates random discrete values

$$
X_0, X_1, \dots, X_n,
$$

which approximate a stochastic trajectory solving the SDE.
